Abstract
--------

Ensemble-based modelling of the atmospheric dispersal of volcanic clouds enables more realistic forecasts by explicitly accounting for uncertainties in eruption source parameters, meteorological data, and systematic errors in transport models. Many ensemble applications, including forecast uncertainty quantification, data assimilation, and probabilistic hazard assessment, require large ensembles to reduce sampling errors and adequately represent probability distributions. However, simulating and processing large ensembles with atmospheric dispersal models can be computationally demanding, even on high-performance computing clusters. Generative AI offers a complementary approach by representing simulated fields in a lower-dimensional latent space, enabling new samples to be generated at low computational cost and facilitating the manipulation of large ensembles. A Variational AutoEncoder (VAE) is trained on ensembles of physics-based simulations and used to generate new samples. The learned latent-space representation is then used to test two ensemble-based data assimilation methods, demonstrating the potential benefits of performing data assimilation in a lower-dimensional space. As a proof of concept, the data assimilation problem is formulated in two dimensions. Two numerical experiments demonstrate that VAE-generated ensembles can reproduce key statistics of a larger reference ensemble and improve agreement with satellite retrievals. The VAE achieves up to a 1000-fold compression of the original fields into 16- and 32-dimensional latent spaces. The VAE-generated ensemble of 4096 members reduces RMSE by 4% relative to the model prior, while latent-space data assimilation further reduces RMSE by 30%. These results highlight the potential of generative AI to efficiently augment physics-based ensembles and improve forecasting skill through latent-space data assimilation.

Short summary
-------------

Forecasting volcanic clouds is challenging because it requires many computationally demanding simulations to capture uncertainty. We use artificial intelligence to generate large numbers of realistic forecasts quickly and inexpensively. Tests show that this approach can reproduce the range of possible volcanic cloud behaviours and improve predictions when combined with observations. This could help scientists and decision-makers better understand volcanic hazards.
