\section{Latent-space data assimilation} \label{sec:da}
Data assimilation (DA) techniques aim to obtain an optimal state of a dynamical
system by combining model forecasts with observations and are widely used by
operational centers to forecast geophysical systems \citep{carrasi2018}.
However, the application of DA to geophysical systems is challenging because
system states are typically represented by very high-dimensional vectors. As
the dimensionality of the system increases, DA methods can become increasingly
difficult to apply due to the complexity of representing the system state and
its associated uncertainties.
%
These limitations are particularly relevant for atmospheric applications, where
the system state may comprise millions of variables, while nonlinear dynamics
and non-Gaussian error distributions can limit the applicability of traditional
DA approaches. For example, many variational and Kalman filter methods rely on
assumptions of approximately Gaussian error statistics, leading to inaccurate
or overconfident state estimates when the underlying distributions are strongly
non-Gaussian or multimodal.
%
Particularly challenging for DA are variables that are lower-bounded and poorly
described by Gaussian distributions, such as water-vapour mixing ratios
\citep{kliewer2016}, rainfall \citep{husak2007}, aerosol concentrations
\citep{oneill2000}, and volcanic ash concentrations \citep{mingari2023}, 
often exhibiting strongly skewed and near-zero distributions.
%
Particle filter avoid the Gaussian assumption by representing the state
distribution with a set of weighted samples (particles) and updating their
weights according to the observation likelihood. However, as the number of
state variables grows, the particle weights can collapse onto one or a few
particles, leading to severe particle degeneracy.

Ensemble-based DA methods partially address the high-dimensionality problem by
representing the uncertainty of the system state through a finite ensemble of
model realizations. This ensemble can be used to approximate the relevant error
statistics and provide a practical framework for applying DA to
high-dimensional, nonlinear geophysical systems.
%
Still, computationally expensive physical models constrain the ensemble size
that can be generated in geoscientific applications, increasing sampling errors
in the estimation of ensemble statistics and potentially leading to suboptimal
analyses. The low-dimensional manifold learned by the VAE can be exploited to
perform data assimilation in a compressed representation of the model state,
expressed in terms of a relatively small number of latent variables while
retaining the main spatial structures and variability of the original fields.
Thus, rather than applying DA directly to the high-dimensional ash
concentration fields, the analysis is performed in latent space and the
resulting states are mapped back to the physical space through the VAE decoder,
substantially reducing the dimensionality of the problem.
%
The application of ensemble-based DA methods in the VAE latent space offers
several advantages. The low dimensionality of the latent representation
substantially reduces the computational cost of the DA analysis, allowing 
larger ensembles to be used than would be practical in the original
physical space. In turn, larger ensembles can potentially reduce sampling
errors in the estimation of ensemble statistics. Moreover, the compact latent
representation facilitates the estimation of these statistics, while the
approximately Gaussian distribution of the latent variables makes the latent
space particularly suitable for ensemble-based DA methods based on Gaussian
assumptions. In this work, we therefore investigate two ensemble-based DA
approaches operating in the VAE latent space, briefly described below: the
Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter (ETKF) and the Particle Filter (PF).

\subsection{Ensemble formulation}
Let $\mathbf{z}_i$ ($i=1\dots n_e$) denote a prior ensemble of $n_e$
latent-space vectors, with $\mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k}$, and let $\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{obs}} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ denote the vector of $m$ observations. The latent-space ensemble is mapped to the physical space through the VAE decoder $D$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_i = D(\mathbf{z}_i)
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$. The corresponding observation-space
ensemble is then obtained by applying the observation operator, 
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}_i = H(\mathbf{x}_i) = H(D(\mathbf{z}_i)),
\end{equation}
where $H$ maps the physical state to the observation space. Thus, although the
analysis is performed in the $k$-dimensional latent space, the observations are
related to the latent variables through the composition of the VAE decoder and
the observation operator. The prior ensemble mean in latent space is defined
as
\begin{equation}
\overline{\mathbf{z}} =
\frac{1}{n_e}\sum_{i=1}^{n_e}\mathbf{z}_i
\end{equation}

\subsection{Ensemble transform Kalman filter} \label{sec:etkf}
We define the background anomaly matrix $\mathbf{Z'} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times
n_e}$, whose $i$-th column represents the deviation of the corresponding
ensemble member from the ensemble mean, $\mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{\bar{z}}$.
Similarly, we define the observation-space anomaly matrix $\mathbf{Y'} \in
\mathbb{R}^{m \times n_e}$, whose columns represent the deviations of the
observation-space ensemble members from the corresponding ensemble mean,
$\mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{\bar{y}}$. The Ensemble transform Kalman filter (ETKF),
introduced by \cite{bishop2001}, is employed in this work following the
formulation adopted by \cite{hunt2007}.  The analysis ensemble is obtained by
updating both the ensemble mean and the ensemble anomalies. The analysis mean
is expressed as a linear combination of the background ensemble anomalies,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{\bar{z}_a} = \mathbf{\bar{z}} + \mathbf{Z'}\,\mathbf{w},
\label{eq:analysis_mean}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_e}$ is a vector of weights to be determined.
The analysis anomalies are obtained by applying a transformation matrix
$\mathbf{W}$ to the background anomalies,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Z'}_a = \mathbf{Z'}\,\mathbf{W}.
\label{eq:analysis_anomalies}
\end{equation}

The weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ in \eqref{eq:analysis_mean} is obtained by
solving the linear system
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{Y'}^\intercal\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{d},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{R}$ is the observation-error covariance matrix, $\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{obs}}-
\mathbf{\bar{y}}$ is the innovation vector, and the matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A} = (k-1)\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Y'}^\intercal\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{Y'}.
\end{equation}

The analysis covariance in the latent space is given by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}^a = \mathbf{Z'} \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{Z'}^\intercal
\label{eq:analysis_covariance}
\end{equation}
To obtain an ensemble with this covariance, the transformation matrix $\mathbf{W}$ in \eqref{eq:analysis_anomalies} is constructed as a symmetric square root of $(k-1)\mathbf{A}^{-1}$. Specifically, using the eigendecomposition $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{V} \mathbf{\Lambda} \mathbf{V}^\intercal$ the transformation matrix is given by

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{W} = \sqrt{k-1}\, \mathbf{V} \mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1/2} \mathbf{V}^\intercal
\end{equation}
This transformation ensures that the sample covariance of the analysis ensemble
is consistent with $\mathbf{P}^a$. The posterior latent ensemble members are
obtained from \eqref{eq:analysis_mean} and \eqref{eq:analysis_anomalies} by
adding the transformed anomalies to the analysis mean. Finally, the resulting
set of vectors $\mathbf{z}_a$ are then be passed through the VAE decoder to obtain the corresponding physical-space ensemble.

\subsection{Regularized bootstrap particle filter} \label{sec:pf}
Particle filtering \citep{doucet2001} has been successfully applied to
low-dimensional models, \eg in nonlinear tracking problems
\citep{arulampalam2002}, but direct application of the basic PF to
high-dimensional systems is prone to weight collapse, as discussed above. As
a result, several variants have been proposed to enable the application of
particle filters in geophysical systems \citep{vanleeuwen2009}. Since the
dimensionality of the latent space is substantially lower than that of the
original physical space, we explore whether particle filtering becomes a viable
option for data assimilation in this reduced representation. In this work,
a basic bootstrap particle filter \citep{gordon1993} with Gaussian kernel
regularization \citep{pham2001} is also tested for latent-space data
assimilation. The particle weights are computed from the observation
likelihood,
\begin{equation}
w_i \propto p\left(\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid\ \mathbf{y}_i\right),
\end{equation}
and normalized to sum to one. Particles are then resampled according to their
weights using systematic resampling \citep{kitagawa1996}, producing an equally
weighted ensemble of size $n_e$. When the particle weights are highly
concentrated on a small number of particles, resampling can substantially
reduce the diversity of the ensemble. To restore particle diversity, the
resampled particles, $\mathbf{z}_i^{r}$, are subsequently perturbed using
a Gaussian kernel,

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_i^{a}=\mathbf{z}_i^{r}+\mathbf{\epsilon}_i, \qquad
\mathbf{\epsilon}_i\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{reg}}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{reg}}=h^2\mathbf{P}$, with $\mathbf{P}$ the sample covariance of the prior latent ensemble and $h$ a prescribed regularization factor. This kernel perturbation provides a simple regularization aimed at increasing the diversity among the resampled particles.
